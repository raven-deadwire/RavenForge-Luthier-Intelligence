A01 — From Mechanical Vibration to Electrical Signal

From string–structure coupling to magnetic transduction and electrical output

Document Purpose
Define the output signal of electric guitars and basses not as the sum of the “tone” of individual components, but as one coupled signal system extending from player excitation through string–structure coupling, pickup-position spatial sampling, magnetic-circuit flux modulation, and the electrical response of the coil and external load. This entry does not establish a single correct pickup position or pot value; it builds a common RavenForge reference model for isolating and validating variables in A02–A05 and C01–C02.

1. Research Question — Where Does the Signal of an Electric Instrument Begin, and Where Is It Altered?

The final output of an electric guitar or bass is not generated independently by any single component. When the player imparts an initial displacement and velocity to the string, the string vibrates under the conditions set by tension, length, linear mass density, and stiffness, while exchanging energy with the neck/body structure through the nut/fret and bridge. Components of the resulting string motion at the pickup location alter the magnetic circuit and induce a voltage in the coil. Pickup impedance and control/cable/input loading then reshape the frequency response [1–7].

Key Perspective
Material does not travel into the pickup. Material and structure change the boundary conditions and mechanical admittance of string dynamics; the pickup reads the resulting change in string motion.

The existing `material → vibration → pickup → output` chain can therefore remain as an internal shorthand, but design and measurement documents expand it to `excitation → coupled string/structure dynamics → pickup-coordinate sampling → magnetic transduction → pickup impedance → electrical loading → output`.

2. String — From Ideal Standing Waves to Real Instrument Strings

2.1 Ideal Stretched String — Scale, Tension, Linear Density

For an ideal flexible string, the transverse-wave velocity is c = √(T/μ), and when the speaking length is L the natural frequencies are expressed as fₙ = (n/2L)√(T/μ). Here, T is tension, μ is linear mass density, and n is the mode number [1]. This relation is RavenForge’s mechanical baseline for understanding how scale length, string mass, and tension are linked at a given pitch.

An ideal mode shape can be represented by sin(nπx/L), with each partial having different node and antinode locations. The string’s “entire sound” is therefore not identical at every point in space.

Figure 2. Basic concept of ideal string modes and pickup spatial sampling.

Idealized point-sampling schematic. Real pickups have finite sensing width and a nonuniform magnetic field [1,5,6].

2.2 Real String — Stiffness, Damping, Termination

A real steel guitar/bass string is not an ideal flexible string. Bending stiffness, core/wrap construction, internal and aerodynamic damping, fret/nut/bridge termination, and coupling to the instrument structure can cause partial frequencies and decay to depart from the ideal model. Stiffness in particular produces inharmonicity by shifting partials away from exact integer multiples [1].

Design rule
RavenForge does not record string response as a single “sustain” value. At minimum, fundamental / harmonic amplitude / partial-specific decay are recorded separately.

3. String–Structure Coupling — Boundary Condition and Energy Sink

The two ends of the string are not ideal fixed points. The neck, body, and hardware can move through the bridge and nut/fret terminations, and when structural mobility is high at a given frequency, string energy can be transferred more strongly into the structure. Driving-point mechanical admittance can be written as Y(ω)=V(ω)/F(ω), while the conductance Re{Y} is related to energy transferred into and dissipated by the structure [2,3].

Paté, Le Carrou & Fabre predicted the decay of solid-body electric-guitar tones by combining isolated-string damping with neck coupling, while Fleischer measured the relationship between high neck conductance at dead spots and short string decay [2,3]. This supports `material + geometry + joint + hardware → structural admittance → string modal amplitude/decay` as a more defensible path than the claim that “wood goes directly into the pickup.”

Design rule
Do not predict tone directly from a species name. Measure the assembly’s modal frequency, damping, FRF/admittance, and actual string partial decay.

4. Pickup Position — A Spatial-Sampling Coordinate, Not a Tone Adjective

A magnetic pickup is not a microphone that directly measures air pressure or body vibration. It senses the motion of a ferromagnetic string moving within a magnetic field. For an ideal point sensor, the position coefficient of the nth mode can be approximated as Hpos(n,xp) ∝ sin(nπxp/L) [5,6]. A pickup located near the node of a given harmonic can therefore suppress that component relative to others.

From this perspective, “the bridge pickup is bright” is only shorthand for a result, not the design variable itself. Moving toward the bridge creates conditions in which the displacement of the fundamental and lower partials becomes relatively smaller, altering the upper-partial ratio. Experimental studies likewise show that pickup position and plucking point leave strong signatures in the spectrum [5,6].

A01 scope
This entry establishes only that pickup position is a spatial-sampling variable. Millimeter-level coordinates, fret-dependent normalized x/L, harmonic-null maps, and finite sensing-width calculations are handed off to A02 and C01.

5. Magnetic Pickup — From the Mechanical Domain to the Electrical Domain

The magnetic pickup is the electromechanical transducer in the A01 signal chain. Faraday’s relation can be written as e(t) = −N·dΦ/dt, but reducing a real guitar pickup to “a metal string moving beside a coil to generate electricity” omits the structure of the magnetic circuit. The permanent magnet, pole/blade, ferromagnetic string, and coil form a single magnetic system, and changes in string position alter the coil-linked flux [4,7].

Horton & Moore modeled the position dependence and nonlinearity of pickup response, while Lemarquand & Lemarquand treated the magnetic circuit of permanent-magnet pickups as a calculable system [4,7]. A sufficiently strong and close magnet can exert back-action on string motion, so the pickup should not be treated as a completely non-invasive sensor.

Design rule
Magnetic transduction is a different problem from pickup coordinate, and also from electrical filtering. RavenForge measurements record string–magnet gap and magnet/pole geometry as separate variables.

6. Electrical Boundary — After Induced Voltage, the Problem Is Impedance

Once an induced EMF has been generated, the pickup coil’s resistance, inductance, and winding/self capacitance form an electrical transfer function, to which the pot, tone network, cable capacitance, and amp/buffer input impedance are added. The pickup output is therefore not simply the “Faraday voltage” itself [5,8].

In a recent controlled winding study, changing the turn count and wire gauge within the same basic magnetic architecture shifted both the self-resonant frequency and the impedance peak [8]. DCR alone should therefore not be used as a proxy for pickup brightness, output, or resonance.

A01 boundary
Detailed R/L/C and resonance/Q are separated into A03, pot/cable loading into A04, and buffer/input impedance into A05. A01 focuses on defining the boundaries between the mechanical → electromagnetic → electrical domains.

8. RavenForge Application — ASKR, EMBLA, Future 3PU

8.1 ASKR — Reframing the Bridge-Near Pickup as a Target-Response Problem

ASKR’s aggressive character is not reduced to the convention of “placing the pickup as close to the bridge as possible.” A02/C01 first define fundamental suppression, upper-partial ratio, harmonic-null avoidance, and attack spectrum as target responses, then derive the coordinate from those targets. In other words: `concept → target response → pickup coordinate`.

8.2 EMBLA — Treating Passive / Buffered Architecture as a Loading Problem

In EMBLA, the difference between passive and active is not simply whether a preamp is present. With a passive high-impedance pickup, cable/load interaction can become part of the voicing, while a high-Z buffer placed immediately after the pickup can largely isolate the downstream cable. C02/A05 therefore distinguish the “loading character to retain” from the “variability to remove.”

8.3 Future 3PU — Designing the Three Pickups as Distinct Spectral Samplers

Future 3PU layouts will be positioned according to normalized coordinate, harmonic-null overlap, and spectral complementarity rather than the conventional neck/middle/bridge labels. Treating the three pickups as distinct spatial samplers of the same string is the starting point for a multi-pickup architecture.

11. Current Conclusion — The Instrument Is a Coupled Signal System

A01 is not intended to create a new hierarchy of “tone.” The output of an electric string instrument is the result of player excitation, coupled string/structure dynamics, spatial sampling, magnetic transduction, pickup impedance, and external loading acting in sequence. Material, pickup position, magnet, winding, pot, and cable are therefore not grouped together as the same kind of tone variable. Each occupies a different causal position and requires a different measurement method.

Current design position
The string is the mechanical carrier. The pickup is the electromechanical boundary. The circuit is the electrical filter. The instrument is the coupled system.

RavenForge’s existing shorthand `material → vibration → pickup → output` will henceforth be expanded in research documents to `excitation → coupled string/structure dynamics → pickup-coordinate sampling → magnetic transduction → pickup impedance → electrical loading → output`.
