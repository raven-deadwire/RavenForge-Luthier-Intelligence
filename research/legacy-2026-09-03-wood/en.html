ASKR — Why Ash, and Why the Fingerboard Remains an Open Variable?

From long-scale structural control to Mpingo / Kingwood candidates

Document purpose
This document applies to ASKR the principle established in Material & Acoustics Research Log 01: evaluate the mechanical properties of the finished structure rather than relying on species names. It explains why an Ash body and a 5-piece laminated neck form the baseline, why Mpingo (African Blackwood) and Kingwood are the highest-priority natural fingerboard candidates, and why their supply and CITES-management constraints matter. It also records RavenForge workshop experience with Ebonol- and Richlite-type synthetic fingerboards and distinguishes the lack of a meaningful advantage over Ebony/Wenge so far as a workshop observation rather than an experimental result.

1. Starting Point — In ASKR, “Aggression” Is a System Requirement, Not a Species Name

In the current RavenForge configurator, ASKR is defined as “Premium & Aggressive,” with a 35-inch extra-long scale as standard and a 37–34-inch multiscale as an extended option [R1]. The current pickup/preamp baseline is the Fishman Fluence system, and the RavenForge modern asymmetrical neck profile is standard [R1]. The design problem is therefore not simply choosing “a wood that suits metal,” but integrating a long scale, down-tuning, a slim and fast neck feel, and a high-output electronic system into one mechanical platform.

With the same string gauge and pitch, increasing scale length raises the required string tension; in a real instrument, however, gauge, tuning, action, and break angle can change at the same time. The design therefore does not assume that “37 inches automatically produces a particular tone.” Instead, the neck assembly is brought into a predictable range of stiffness, mass, and modal response, and the effects of the body and fingerboard on that structure are treated as measurement targets.

Core perspective
ASKR’s aggression is not a property created by species names such as Ash or Ebony. Geometry and assembly are controlled first to pursue structural consistency at long scale, low unwanted vibrational loss, and fast playing response; wood is then used as a material-property variable in service of those targets.

1.1 Closer to “Boundary-Condition Design” Than “Tonewood Selection”

As summarized in Research Log 01, string energy couples to the instrument structure at the fret/nut side and at the bridge side. In ASKR, making those boundary conditions as consistent as possible makes it easier to evaluate pickups, preamps, string gauges, and setup as separate variables [R2]. The body therefore uses Ash as a reference baseline, neck variability is reduced through lamination, and the fingerboard is screened independently as a high-density, high-stiffness, wear-resistant outer layer.

2. Body — Why Ash Is the ASKR Baseline

The current ASKR standard body in the configurator is a 3-piece solid construction with Ash specified for both the center core and the side wings [R1]. This is not an adoption of the traditional shorthand “Ash is bright and tight” as a scientific fact. Instead, Ash is used as an obtainable structural material that can be standardized by measuring density and stiffness blank by blank.

2.1 Controlled Tests Show That Structural Response Can Change — Not That “Ash Is Always Better”

Ray, Kaljun, and Straže (2021) compared instruments using the same neck, bridge, pickups, and strings while changing only the body between European Ash (Fraxinus excelsior) and Walnut. In that test, the stiffer Ash body shifted modal frequencies upward, showed lower damping than Walnut, and produced longer decay in some open-string harmonics [1]. This is direct evidence that body-material properties can influence bridge-side structural response.

However, the instrument comparison used only one body per species and specific support/open-string conditions [1]. RavenForge therefore does not take away the conclusion “Ash = longer sustain.” The useful conclusion is methodological: record the density, stiffness, and damping of each body blank, then evaluate the finished instrument’s FRF and decay together.

3. Neck — At Long Scale, Lamination Is for Variability Management, Not “Tone Layers”

The current ASKR baseline neck is a 5-piece laminated construction using the Ash / Maple / Wenge material family [R1]. On a 35-inch or 37–34-inch platform with a modern asymmetrical carve, the requirement is not simply to make the neck thicker, but to manage stiffness and torsional behavior of the finished neck assembly while preserving the intended playing feel.

Lamination does not automatically increase EI merely because more glue lines are present. With the same material, section, and grain direction, the number of pieces alone does not guarantee higher stiffness [R2]. ASKR uses five pieces as the baseline because each lamella can be screened for grain runout, density, moisture state, and a resonance proxy, while left/right mass and stiffness distribution can be balanced.

Design rule
The ASKR neck does not assign a tonal role to each stripe, such as “Bloodwood for highs” or “Wenge for mids.” Lamellae are structural members used to meet target stiffness, mass, symmetry, and manufacturability; final evaluation is based on the completed neck’s modal frequencies, damping, fret-by-fret conductance, and dead-spot locations.

3.1 Because the Neck Matters, Species Character Is Controlled First

Research on dead spots and decay in solid-body instruments repeatedly shows that coupling between neck resonances and string partials can have a large effect [R2]. Treating the neck as a lower-priority “tonewood selection” area in ASKR therefore does not mean its acoustic influence is small. The strategy is the opposite: first bring a highly influential structure into a predictable range, then observe the differences introduced by the fingerboard and body.

4. Fingerboard — Mpingo and Kingwood Are the Highest-Priority Natural Candidates

In the current configurator, Wenge is the ASKR standard fingerboard and Ebony is the recommended alternative [R1]. From RavenForge’s material-research perspective, however, the next highest-priority natural candidates are Mpingo / African Blackwood (Dalbergia melanoxylon) and Kingwood (Dalbergia cearensis). This priority does not mean that either species has already been shown to “sound better.” It means their high density, stiffness, hardness, and potential contribution as an outer structural layer are the properties to test first in matched A/B prototypes.

4.1 Mpingo — The High-Density Candidate with the Strongest Current Evidence

Liu et al. compared Ebony, Indian Rosewood, African Blackwood (Mpingo), and Hard Maple under the same experimental protocol. In their samples, African Blackwood had the highest air-dry density at 1.36 g/cm³, an MOE of 17.9 GPa, and an MOR of 190.8 MPa, while showing the lowest moisture-induced swelling among the four species [2]. Macro-hardness in the three measured directions was also high at 15.5 / 18.7 / 16.5 kN, and wear resistance met the criteria considered suitable for fretboard material [2].

These results make Mpingo mechanically compelling as an ASKR fingerboard candidate. A high-density board, however, increases neck-assembly mass and can shift modal frequencies, so high property values do not automatically guarantee better electrical output or longer sustain. The “high-E / high-ρ outer layer” hypothesis must be tested through actual neck conductance and string decay.

4.2 Kingwood — Mechanically Attractive, but Direct Fretboard Evidence Is More Limited

Kingwood is an attractive Dalbergia for small fretboard stock because of its very high density, high hardness, and fine, even texture. The Decorative Hardwoods Association Veneer Species Guide gives a representative specific gravity of about 1.20 and an MOE of about 2.62 million psi (approximately 18.1 GPa) [4]. Unlike Mpingo, however, this review did not identify a study that directly compares Kingwood with Ebony/Maple under a fretboard-specific protocol.

Kingwood therefore remains a high-priority candidate, but it is not treated as a “proven upgrade over Ebony.” Available blank width, length, defects, oiliness, bonding behavior, and machinability must be checked on actual stock before it is used in a prototype.

5. Supply Reality — Why Mpingo and Kingwood Are Difficult to Fix as Standard Materials

Mpingo and Kingwood both belong to the genus Dalbergia, and Dalbergia spp. other than D. nigra fall under CITES Appendix II Annotation #15. The current annotation exempts finished musical instruments, finished musical-instrument parts/accessories, and certain finished products below specified weight thresholds, but international trade in raw logs, sawn wood, or unfinished fingerboard blanks is not automatically exempt [5]. Shipping a completed bass and repeatedly importing raw blanks for production are therefore not the same regulatory problem.

For Kingwood, Brazilian native-wood export procedures and CITES licensing must both be considered; IBAMA separately administers export authorization for native wood products and the CITES licensing process [6]. Mpingo likewise requires attention to a limited East African supply chain and conservation/legal-origin documentation. Both species are therefore better managed as stock-dependent research/custom materials with verified provenance and paperwork than as casual “exotic options whenever available.”

5.1 This Does Not Reduce the Roles of Ebony and Wenge

Ebony remains a fingerboard reference material because of its high hardness, density, and accumulated lutherie experience. In the Liu et al. experiment, Ebony also showed high hardness and MOE, supporting its role as a traditional benchmark [2]. Mpingo showed greater dimensional stability and stronger values in some mechanical metrics in that study, but there is no direct evidence that this translates into acoustic “superiority” on an electric bass.

Wenge remains the current ASKR standard [R1]. This is not because it is considered acoustically “below Mpingo,” but because it provides a production baseline around which repeatable data can be accumulated, including sourcing availability, machining experience, cost, and compatibility with the neck-lamination system. Separating the standard material from the highest-priority research candidate is not a design contradiction; it separates production from research.

6. Synthetic Fingerboards — Why Ebonol and Richlite Were Not Adopted as Standard

The reasons for evaluating synthetic fingerboards were reasonable: reduce species- and specimen-level variability, lessen dependence on scarce tropical woods and international-trade paperwork, and obtain a repeatable black surface and stable dimensions. Ebonol is described as a laminate of black paper and phenolic resin [7], while Richlite is a paper composite made by impregnating paper with thermosetting resin and consolidating it under heat and pressure [8]. Both are therefore fiber/resin laminates rather than simply “plastic imitations of wood.”

6.1 RavenForge Workshop Observation — The Advantage Was Not Large Enough to Displace Ebony

Workshop observation · not a controlled study
In previous hands-on use and machining of Ebonol- and Richlite-type synthetic fingerboards, I did not obtain a repeatable advantage in response, sustain, or tonal behavior over Ebony large enough to justify choosing the material specifically for that reason. Nor did storage, surface care, later machining, and repair feel overwhelmingly easier than with natural wood. This is a record of workshop experience, not a blinded A/B test in which only the fingerboard was changed on otherwise identical necks, and it should not be expanded into evidence of scientific superiority or inferiority.

Manufacturer descriptions of Ebonol as “tight / bright” may be useful as product descriptions, but they are not controlled evidence that it outperforms Ebony in ASKR pickup output, decay, or FRF [7]. Richlite is likewise documented by its manufacturer as uniform and machinable [8], but this review did not identify a peer-reviewed A/B study directly comparing its electric-bass fingerboard modal response with Ebony under the same protocol.

The current conclusion is therefore not that “synthetic materials are bad.” They can be reconsidered for separate purposes such as sourcing ethics, color consistency, specific customer requirements, or a fretless wear surface. For ASKR, however, there is not yet a reason to replace high-density natural fingerboards without a clear acoustic, structural, or maintenance gain.

7. ASKR #001 — How Should an Early Prototype That Differs from the Current Standard Be Used?

The ASKR #001 custom quote records a 37–34-inch multiscale, a 1-piece chambered Ash body, Flame Maple veneer, Wenge fingerboard, 5-piece Maple/Wenge neck, EMG TW45 pickups, and a Darkglass Tone Capsule [R3]. The current configurator standard is a 35-inch scale, 3-piece Ash/Ash body, 5-piece Ash/Maple/Wenge family, Wenge standard / Ebony recommended reference, and Fishman Fluence system [R1].

The completed #001 therefore must not be interpreted as single-variable evidence that “Ash was correct” or “Wenge was correct.” It remains highly valuable as an integrated prototype baseline showing how body architecture, neck family, scale, and electronics operate together relative to the current standard.

7.1 The Next Prototype Should Get Closer to “Changing Only the Fingerboard”

To validate fingerboard candidates, matched necks that retain the same profile, truss rod, reinforcement, and fret geometry are more useful than an ASKR #001-type prototype in which several variables change at once. Wenge / Ebony / Mpingo should be compared first, with Kingwood added as a fourth candidate when legal stock of sufficient dimensions can be secured. Ebonol/Richlite should be revisited as separate matched synthetic samples only if there is a reason to quantify the earlier workshop experience.

10. Current Conclusion — ASKR Materials Build a Controlled Structure, Not an “Aggressive Tonewood”

In ASKR, Ash is not a species that promises a particular tone; it is a baseline material for repeatable measurement of the body-side structure. The 5-piece laminated neck is likewise not a story about being more premium or stronger because it has more stripes, but a manufacturing method for controlling neck mass, stiffness, and torsional variability first on the 35-inch and 37–34-inch platforms.

For the fingerboard, Mpingo and Kingwood are the highest-priority natural research candidates. Mpingo has the strongest current evidence because direct fretboard-material research shows high density, MOE, hardness, and strong dimensional stability [2]. Kingwood is likewise a dense, stiff candidate, but comparable direct data are more limited [4]. Neither species is fixed as a current standard specification because of Dalbergia trade requirements and the difficulty of maintaining stable stock [5,6].

Ebony remains the reference comparator, while Wenge is the current production baseline. Ebonol- and Richlite-type materials have the potential advantage of material consistency, but RavenForge’s practical experience has not shown a meaningful response or maintenance advantage large enough to replace Ebony. Synthetic materials are therefore not adopted by default simply because they are “advanced materials”; they will be tested again when a use case emerges in which a measurable advantage can be demonstrated.

Current design position
ASKR does not ask which material sounds the most aggressive. It asks which structure is predictable enough that material changes can be measured.

Preferred natural research candidates: Mpingo / Kingwood
Reference: Ebony
Production baseline: Wenge
Synthetic materials: experimental only, until a measurable advantage is demonstrated.
