EMBLA & ASKR: Toward a Luthiery of Mythic Matter, Philosophical Organology, and Active Mediation

Raven Cho (Yonsei University, College of Liberal Arts, Bachelor of Philosophy)

Abstract

This essay examines how the storytelling of the Norse creation myth embedded in RavenForge’s electric bass prototypes, EMBLA and ASKR, can be organically interwoven with modern materials engineering and acoustic design in instrument making. To analyze the process by which driftwood, as passive matter, becomes an entity capable of actively producing sound once it encounters a performer, the essay draws on philosophical organology and new-materialist thought. On this basis, the ASKR model is read through Sheldon Dingwall’s philosophy of the multiscale ecosystem and sonic clarity, foregrounding the realization of extreme punch and rational structure. EMBLA, by contrast, is grounded in Gerald Marleaux’s notion of organic resonance, the Regio Tone Wood (RTW) philosophy, and the legacy of the award-winning Consat model, focusing on the production of ecological balance and a warm acoustic texture. Ultimately, the essay argues that these two instruments are not mere industrial commodities but active mediating agents that are completed only when the individual narrative of the performer is added to them.3

1. Introduction: Reinterpreting the Norse Creation Myth and the Ontological Meaning of Lutherie

Ancient myths that explain the origin of humanity do not remain at the level of fantasy alone. They continue to serve as profound sources of thought because they function as an ultimate metaphor for poiesis: the inscription of intelligence and vitality into otherwise meaningless fragments of nature. In the Norse creation accounts found in Völuspá of the Poetic Edda and in Snorri Sturluson’s Prose Edda, the first human beings are not born from splendid metals or flawless jewels. Odin, Hönir, and Lothur walk along the seashore and discover two rough pieces of driftwood washed in by the tide: fragments of ash and elm. Through the touch of the three gods, these nameless pieces of timber are finally granted destiny and reborn as the first humans, Askr and Embla. At the very moment when mere fragments of nature are given immense meaning and function, the breath of the creation myth becomes vividly palpable.1

The author finds that the three gifts bestowed by the gods in this mythic scene of creation correspond strikingly to the key engineering elements embedded in the making of the modern electric bass. The ‘soul’ breathed in by Odin resembles the electromagnetic vitality of active preamps and pickup systems that animate cold timber. The ‘sense and reason’ conferred by Hönir, god of wisdom and prophecy, are realized in rigorous geometry, structural engineering, and neck ergonomics designed so that instrument and performer may operate as one. Finally, the ‘warmth and living color’ given by Lothur—often understood as a fire god and an archaic precursor of Loki—may be discerned in the psychoacoustic resonance of tonewood, which expands string vibration into warm overtones, and in the visual beauty of resin-joined archtop structures. It is a process in which mythic vitality settles upon cold matter and artistic warmth is added to it.1

According to the structural anthropology of Claude Lévi-Strauss, myth is a tool of thought through which human beings circumvent or reconcile unwelcome contradictions.6 Between wood as wilderness and electronic circuitry as the condensed form of civilization, or between organic vibration and the cold resolution of the digital age, there remains a gap that is not easily bridged. Yet in the RavenForge workshop, these worlds can, one hopes, be fused through the mediating figure of the instrument. Inspired by Askr and Embla, the RavenForge lineup thus becomes a third referent, binding nature and advanced technology into a new artistic language.6

The instrument envisioned here aims to contain, simultaneously, both mythic philosophy and the performer’s improvised sound, much like Saussure’s dual structure of langue and parole: the relation between enduring pattern and momentary expression. The result is not a mere assembly of manufactured parts but a high-order cultural practice in which silent fragments of nature are endowed with physical vibration and cultural meaning, and reborn as active actants.6

2. The Semiotics of Matter and New Materialisms: Vibrant Substance and Material Flow

2.1. Vibrant Matter and Thing-Power

Any serious inquiry into the philosophy of instrument making must first dismantle the anthropocentric view that treats matter solely as passive or inert stuff at the disposal of human purposes. In Vibrant Matter, Jane Bennett criticizes the modern partition of the sensible that divides the world into dull matter as object and lively life as subject. For Bennett, matter is not a mere background but a quasi-agent, an actant endowed with its own trajectories, tendencies, and capacities to redirect events. Just as omega-3 fatty acids alter human mood, active chemicals and methane from landfills transform environments, and the Amazonian earthworms observed by Charles Darwin and Bruno Latour reconfigure soil chemistry and the very boundary between forest and savanna, nonhuman matter also works actively at the center of major ecological and physical transformations.7

Viewed through Bennett’s concept of distributive agency, indebted to Spinoza’s account of conative bodies and Adorno’s philosophy of nonidentity, the electric bass may be understood as a site in which metal strings, pickup magnets, and natural tonewoods participate alongside the human performer on a less vertical plane in the production of musical events. Wood is never fully determined by the luthier’s intention alone; its climate history and porous internal structure answer the performer’s touch with unexpected overtones and resonances, thereby disclosing a vitality of its own.7

2.2. Materials against Materiality and the Trajectory of Flow

Tim Ingold criticizes the Aristotelian legacy of hylomorphism, according to which a human subject imposes form upon otherwise inactive matter. Drawing on James Gibson’s tripartite distinction among medium, substances, and surfaces, Ingold argues that things are not externally given forms but ongoing processes produced, dissolved, and transformed through the turbulence and mixture of materials in flux.8 For Ingold, the essence of materials lies not in fixed attributes but in the histories they have traversed and accumulated.12

RavenForge’s ideal of instrument making likewise follows the new-materialist perspectives of Bennett and Ingold. Rather than suppressing wood into compliance, the luthier works in concert with the material’s own interlocking grain directions, internal damping factors, and latent tendencies, weaving the form of the instrument by entrusting himself to those flows. This is not the magical act of giving life to matter from without, but the restoration of a generative movement already immanent within the material itself.

2.3. The Language of the Producer and the Destruction of Myth

In Roland Barthes’s Mythologies, mass-produced objects in modern society—plastic, or even the then-new Citroën automobile—come to appear as though they were governed by natural law, even though they emerge from a particular capitalist history. This transformation occurs through depoliticized speech. Within such a second-order semiological system, the labor history and sensory materiality once embedded in things gradually vanish, leaving an empty signifier to be passively consumed by the public.13

Barthes nevertheless proposes a way to break the bourgeois frame of myth: the language of the producer. A woodcutter, for example, does not encounter a tree as a romantic image or an object of detached contemplation, but as an operational object directly bound to labor and practice. In that moment, myth can no longer function. RavenForge seeks, likewise, not to be absorbed by the homogeneous logic of CNC routing for mass production, but to assemble semi-automatic jigs by hand wherever possible and to confront the resistance of living tonewood with plane and chisel. Such a choice becomes a small rebellion—indeed a revolution—against the myth of blind commodity consumption. The instrument is reborn, then, not as a brand-sign to be consumed but as an operative thing through which performer and worker jointly create a new musical reality.13

3. Acoustics and Quantum Entanglement: The Psychoacoustics of Wood and Agential Realism

3.1. The Empirical Closure of the Tonewood Debate and Acoustic Demonstration

Remarkably, the active agency of matter emphasized by new materialism no longer remains at the level of speculative philosophy alone. Today, its efficacy is increasingly supported by experiments and studies in acoustic engineering. For a long time, some players argued that, in solid-body electric instruments, wood functions only as a passive support and that sound is determined exclusively by the interaction between the electromagnetic field of the pickup and the metal string.3

However, the 2021 peer-reviewed paper On the Audibility of Electric Guitar Tonewood, published by Jasiński and colleagues at AGH University of Science and Technology in Poland, offers a clear rebuttal to this long-standing myth of material passivity.14 To control all variables other than wood, the researchers built experimental guitars from only four kinds of identically CNC-machined timber: sapele (640.3 kg/m³), rosewood (961.2 kg/m³), pine (466 kg/m³), and plywood (705.4 kg/m³), each with distinct internal structures.14 They also standardized the plucking procedure by modifying a hairpin into a spring-based mechanical device so that specific strings (E2, D3, E4) were excited at exactly the same angle and force. Under these controlled conditions, the recorded signals were analyzed spectrally using a Focusrite Scarlett 18i8 interface and the Python library librosa, yielding meaningful differences that cut against the older commonplaces.14 The study therefore provides a powerful counterargument to extreme positions claiming that wood has virtually no acoustic effect at all.

3.2. Agential Realism: Intra-action

The acoustic agency of wood acquires additional philosophical depth through Karen Barad’s agential realism. Drawing on Niels Bohr’s interpretation of quantum mechanics, Barad argues that subject and object—performer and instrument in this case—do not first exist as separate entities and then interact. Rather, they are products of intra-action: they emerge together, within specific material-discursive arrangements, through mutual entanglement and the co-constitution of boundaries and identities.15

Particularly important here is Barad’s concept of the agential cut. Each time a performer plucks a given string or turns a tone-control knob, innumerable acoustic possibilities collapse into one determinate sound. Other sonic possibilities are thereby constitutively excluded. Musical creation is thus, at its core, an ethics of exclusion and, at the same time, an ethics of inclusion: a practice of deciding what will be admitted and what will be left aside.15

RavenForge’s bass lineup seeks precisely to place that power of the agential cut as sharply as possible in the hands of the performer. Instrument and player define one another while jointly enabling an explosion of creativity; together they form a cybernetic entanglement.3

4. Organology and the Poetics of Space: The Individuation of Machines and Intimate Resonance

An instrument is more than a device for emitting sound. It is a privileged object into which the performer’s psyche and affect sink, and in which human existence itself comes to dwell. The thought of Simondon, Bachelard, and Heidegger allows the essence of the instrument to be articulated with greater depth.

4.1. The Concretization of the Technical Object and the Margin of Indetermination

Gilbert Simondon does not reduce the technical object to a slave of utility or a mere machine. For him, a technical object is the crystallization of human action and effort into functioning structures; it is a universal mediator of human reality and a bridge between nature and culture. What modern society often calls alienation, he suggests, stems not from the machine itself but from our cultural inability to read the human meaning sedimented within it.20

Simondon also questions the common assumption that the perfection of a machine is proportional to its automatism. A closed system of purely passive automation cannot respond flexibly to new circumstances. A truly superior technical object, by contrast, is an open machine endowed with a margin of indetermination: it remains sensitive to subtle external signals and to unforeseeable variations.20

In this sense, RavenForge’s pursuit of high-end active preamps beyond the limits of traditional passive purism is no accident. Active equalization is often treated as a source of coloration, yet in a high-impedance environment it may open a space in which the performer can register local acoustic feedback more delicately while also shaping frequencies with less sonic loss and with greater precision.3

This technological evolution follows what Simondon calls concretization: a process in which primitive and abstract components gradually acquire internal coherence, forming an organic causal chain that begins to resemble natural existence itself. To borrow Simondon’s metaphor, the performer is not a supervisor issuing commands to an inert machine, but the permanent organizer and conductor of a technical ensemble, guiding its overall harmony in sensitive response to its ongoing respiration.20

4.2. The Poetics of Space and the Imagination of the Shell

In The Poetics of Space, Gaston Bachelard investigates not geometric space as such but inhabited space—the kind of space that actively shapes our psychology and imagination. For Bachelard, the house or shelter is not a mere geometric object but a nest that protects reverie and interiority, and thus a metaphor for humanity itself. We seek refuges in order to imagine and in order not to be scattered by the storms of life.21

Bachelard pays special attention to miniature and intimate spaces such as shells, drawers, and boxes. To hide oneself within a shell is to confront solitude and accept it; condensed in such a small space, one paradoxically meets the dimension of intimate immensity. Through the dialectic of outside and inside, the boundaries of the inner world are opened anew. Just as the warmth of a hut becomes more precious when rain and wind intensify outside, so the performer may withdraw from the pressures and noises of the world into the instrument’s resonant chamber—a quiet shell in which the essence of existence is experienced more deeply.21

Bachelard also names the body’s ability to remember the position of a doorknob or stairway through touch as fingertip memory. The concept applies directly to performers: through muscular sensation rather than sight, they spatially internalize the curvature of the fingerboard and the tension of the strings. In this sense, the instrument becomes an instrument of topoanalysis, expanding bodily capability while simultaneously serving as an intimate psychological dwelling.21

4.3. The Thing and the Gathering of the Fourfold

In the later philosophy of Martin Heidegger, the thing is not a mere objectified object but an ontological center that opens a world and gathers meaning. In Building Dwelling Thinking, Heidegger illustrates this through the image of the bridge.22

A bridge is not simply a structure that physically connects two riverbanks. Once a bridge stands over the river, each side ceases to be mere ground and becomes a bank in a newly constituted place. The bridge gathers into one site the essences of earth, sky, mortals, and divinities—the fourfold.22

RavenForge’s electric bass aspires to function in the same way. When an instrument is placed on stage, the memory of earth preserved in the tonewood, the sky-like spread of frequencies, the finite being of the performer who embraces mortality, and the presence of creative inspiration all coexist in one space. Performance is not an act of ownership or domination over the instrument; it is a poetic practice of letting-be, refusing to reduce technology to mere tool-use and allowing the thing to disclose its essence. In this sense, performance is a form of poetic dwelling in which one entrusts oneself to the instrument’s sound and gives new life to the space it opens.26

5. The ASKR Model: Ecological Evolution in Dingwall Hardware and an Extreme Rational Skeleton

In the Norse myth, Odin and his brothers breathe spirit and rational insight into resilient ash, thereby creating the first man, Askr.1 RavenForge’s ASKR line likewise aims at a combative instrument designed around a powerful frame and transparent clarity. The model seeks to inherit the legacy of hardware innovation proposed by the Canadian luthier Sheldon Dingwall.3

5.1. The Dual-Density Body and the Defeat of the Mud Cannon

As modern popular music—and especially modern metal and djent—has developed, the bass guitar has increasingly been asked to extend into extreme low registers. Yet within the density of a large ensemble, those low frequencies often congeal into a dull and muddy mass, the phenomenon Dingwall identified as the ‘mud cannon’ problem. (See the post published on March 29, 2026.)

To overcome this acoustic limitation, ASKR adopts the central orientation of the dual-density body architecture introduced into Dingwall’s production line. In the upper region of the body, where the low strings (B and E) pass, dense swamp ash or North American hard ash is used to establish a firm low-frequency skeleton and rich resonance. In the lower region, where the higher strings (D and G) are situated, lighter and faster-responding woods such as alder are joined in order to allow upper harmonics to radiate more quickly and fluidly. Such ecological integration of heterogeneous woods within a single instrument mediates, in Lévi-Straussian terms, the opposition between low-end density and high-end suppleness.6

5.2. Wave Equations and the 37-inch Multiscale Ecosystem

On a conventional 34-inch scale, the B string has often suffered from inadequate tension and unstable pitch—the so-called ‘spaghetti string’ syndrome. To solve this, Dingwall adopted a 37-inch multiscale fanned-fret system that evolved out of the Novax license. Inspired by the string layout of the grand piano and the harp, the scale length is progressively extended toward the lower strings, reaching as far as 37 inches. The result is extraordinary control of tension and a low register with the unusual clarity of a struck piano’s bottom octave. Added to this are a bridge equipped with independent saddles for each string and a tool-free string-lock system derived from Formula 1 motorsport engineering, both of which push mechanical reliability to an extreme. ASKR seeks to inherit not only the structural innovation of Dingwall hardware but also the intensity of the technical and acoustic thinking through which sonic contradiction is overcome.3

5.3. Narrative Morphology and the Energy Feedback Loop

According to Vladimir Propp’s Morphology of the Folktale, the hero’s narrative follows a syntagmatic sequence: the emergence of lack, the acquisition of a magical agent from a donor, and finally the overcoming of crisis and the restoration of balance.35

On stage, the performer faces a crisis of lack when the bass line risks being severed beneath distortion and drum noise. At this point, ASKR intervenes with the magical agent of a multiband active preamp—whether a Darkglass Tone Capsule in the Dingwall lineage or a reproduction of the HAZ preamp associated with Stuart Spector.3 By cutting unnecessary high-treble information and boosting the crucial midrange zones around 70 Hz, 500 Hz, and 2.8 kHz, the instrument can project an overwhelming attack that pierces the wall of modern metal.3

Once this clarified sonic energy strikes the audience, what Joseph Campbell called the ‘flow of life’ begins to pass dynamically between stage and hall. The enthusiasm of the crowd feeds back toward the player and draws the performer toward a cathartic state of immersion. The energy feedback loop named by Sheldon Dingwall thus reveals a dramatic rite in which performer and audience become quantumly entangled through the mediating presence of ASKR.36

6. The EMBLA Model: The Fusion of Marleaux and KD, Organic Resonance of the Earth, and the Avant-Garde

If ASKR appears as a symbol of masculine creation through the overcoming of limits, EMBLA stands at the opposite pole: an organic yet avant-garde instrument inspired by elm and by the origin of the first woman, Embla.3 It bears the same qualities that once made elm suitable for shipbuilding and bow making: remarkable resistance to moisture and a tenacious vitality. EMBLA seeks to reproduce, in a solid-body form, the warmth and range of the acoustic contrabass by fusing the lutherie philosophies of the German master Gerald Marleaux and the Bulgarian craftsman Kostadin Dimitrov of KD Guitars.3

6.1. The Regio Tone Wood Philosophy and Sustainability

For more than thirty years, Marleaux has pursued a philosophy of perfect balance and organic resonance.3 Like his acclaimed Diva model, EMBLA adopts an ergonomic curvature that reduces the bodily resistance generated when an instrument meets the performer as nearly to zero as possible. Acoustically, the interlocking grain of elm absorbs the strings’ energy into the porous interior of the wood, softening abrasive transient edges and producing a woody after-resonance rich in the low-mid register.3

Most important is the fact that EMBLA practices Marleaux’s Regio Tone Wood philosophy instead of a capitalist logic that consumes nature indiscriminately.3 Rather than importing rare tropical species, it aspires to use Bulgarian elm and Balkan maple that have been air-dried for thirteen to twenty-five years in the regional forests of Lower Saxony or in the clean zones of the Balkan mountains.37 Such an approach, climate-neutral in orientation and reduced in logistical carbon footprint, is also a practical enactment of Jane Bennett’s call for respect toward matter and of a politically ecological sustainability.7

6.2. The Legacy of Consat and Contra and Geometric Ergonomics

In order to mediate between two seemingly opposed goals—acoustic resonance and structural stiffness—EMBLA fully inherits the legacy of two signature Marleaux models that exemplify the essence of German instrument building: the Consat and the Contra. The Marleaux Consat bass received the Deutscher Musikinstrumentenpreis, one of the highest honors awarded by the German Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Technology, while the Contra bass was internationally recognized for its ergonomic perfection and acoustic excellence when it received the Best of Show prize at the 2016 NAMM Show.

The precise chambering techniques and fluid body contouring that contributed to the success of the Consat are carried directly into EMBLA. This curvature, which fits the performer’s body with exceptional intimacy, cancels bodily resistance and allows the player to concentrate fully on intra-action with the material. The philosophy of perfect balance that Marleaux has refined for decades makes possible an extreme reduction of weight while preserving the breath of wood and maintaining the tension required to resist external deformation. The Contra model extends this legacy further by using the same kinds of materials familiar from high-end violins, double basses, and violas, producing an instrument that is dynamic, quick to respond, and rich in harmonic content. In passive mode it yields an acoustic sound suffused with the warmth of wood; in active mode, blended with its characteristic miking system, that inherent timbre is intensified and lifted to a new level.

6.3. The Cone Core Multi-Laminate Neck Structure of KD Guitars

The neck, as the central nervous system of the instrument, pursues extreme stability by adopting KD Guitars’ proprietary Cone Core neck philosophy.3 Bulgarian Balkan flamed maple, dried over decades, is multi-laminated in three or more pieces with very hard reinforcement woods such as purpleheart or mahogany; the fingerboard is made from stabilized wood that suppresses shrinkage and expansion by more than ninety percent, and the nut is formed from Indian water buffalo horn.37

EMBLA also moves freely between a 32-inch medium scale and a 36-inch sub-contra scale, and by incorporating up to 28 frets together with a zero fret, it offers a vast melodic canvas reminiscent of the contrabass.3 Its electronics likewise retain the breath of wood by means of a push-pull passive switch and custom humbuckers in wooden housings based on Haeussel or Delano designs, driving the timber’s natural respiration directly into the amplifier rather than replacing it with artificial coloration.3

7. Transmedia Storytelling and the Mythic Construction of Brand Identity

RavenForge does not stop at listing hardware specifications. Rather, drawing on Douglas B. Holt’s principles of cultural branding, it seeks to sublate the brand into a large-scale identity myth. Holt argues that consumers do not engage brands merely as carriers of functional utility; in periods of rapid change, they embrace them as symbolic metaphors capable of reinforcing their own social and artistic identities. The collective anxiety felt by bassists in the noisy environment of contemporary popular music—the sense of marginalization and endless thirst for tone—is answered by RavenForge through the dramatic narrative borrowed from the Norse creation myth.53

This process of brand construction is intensified through Henry Jenkins’s concept of transmedia storytelling. In a transmedia environment, no single central text explains everything. The player who takes a RavenForge model into hand is invited to investigate the wave mechanics embedded in ASKR’s dual-density construction and the ecological practices embedded in EMBLA’s RTW timber, gathering knowledge across websites, manuals, and online forums like a hunter-collector.54

As such fragments of knowledge accumulate, they generate what Neil Young has described as additive comprehension. The philosophies of Dingwall, Marleaux, and KD Guitars, once separate, are reassembled through the collective intelligence of performers into a unified act of world-building, in Pierre Lévy’s sense.54 What RavenForge ultimately seeks to offer, then, is not an ordinary commodity but a powerful role-playing medium through which the performer may continue unfolding a story like an ancient hero—both in everyday life and, more emphatically, on stage. Within that process, the performer completes a personal worldview and forms a deeper bond with the brand.54

8. Conclusion: The Ultimate Entanglement and Co-evolution of Human, Matter, and Technology

RavenForge’s ASKR and EMBLA lines are not reducible to consumer products tailored merely to market demand. In them one can observe an organological miracle: the silent fragments of driftwood once scattered on the shore, waiting for the breath of the gods, are transformed by modern lutherie and philosophical reflection into beings that finally speak in their own voices.1

As Jasiński’s empirical acoustic research shows, wood is not a passive shell that simply receives string vibration.14 In Jane Bennett’s terms, it is itself a vibrant matter: an active participant that vibrates, lives, and produces events in entanglement with the electromagnetic field.7 ASKR forms a rational skeleton through its firm 37-inch scale and its fusion of dual-density woods, 32 while EMBLA becomes an ultimate shell—an organic refuge in which the performer’s imagination is housed—through Balkan elm, the ergonomically proven balance of the Consat, and the Cone Core neck.37

At every moment when the performer grips the neck and strikes the strings, the intra-action and agential cut emphasized by Karen Barad erupt into a vast physical and quasi-quantum entanglement.15 The performer’s intention, the climatic memory stored in the wood, vibration within the magnetic field, and amplification through the amplifier lose their separate boundaries and become concretized as one musical reality.20 This is not the technocratic disaster in which machines alienate the human, but the scene foreseen by Simondon in which the human returns as conductor of the ensemble and, as Heidegger suggests, the fourfold gathers upon the bridge of the instrument into a form of poetic dwelling.22

RavenForge therefore shatters what Roland Barthes called the passive and depoliticized myth of the commodity, elevating the instrument to a new meaning: the language of the producer and a companion that evolves continuously with the performer. Silent wood, placed upon the altar of the RavenForge world, remains an enduring voice that carries both the most delicate resolution and the warmest vitality, speaking on behalf of human narrative itself.
